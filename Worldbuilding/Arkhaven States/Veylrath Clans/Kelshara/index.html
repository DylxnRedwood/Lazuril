Kelshara¶

Kelshara is a neighbouring settlement north-east of Tethvale, set deeper into the rainforest where the canopy thickens and the old stone of the Veylrain Expanse rises through the roots.

It belongs to the Veylrath Clans, but its reputation is less about trade and more about memory. Kelshara is known for historians, relic preservation, artifact restoration, and careful study of the ruins buried beneath the jungle.

The settlement is built around a series of moss-covered terraces known as the Kethari Steps, though no one agrees who first carved them or why so many of the lower stones disappear during the wet season.

Keepers of Memory¶

Kelshara’s most respected people are not warriors or merchants, but ruin-wardens, oral historians, appraisers, restoration workers, and elders who know how to read age from stone, root, metal, and dust.

Relics found across nearby clan lands are often brought here before they are kept, traded, returned, sealed, or destroyed.

This does not make Kelshara a museum. The Veylrath do not treat old things as decoration. A relic is a question, a warning, a debt, or a danger until it has been properly understood.

Restoration and Relics¶

The settlement contains shaded workhouses where broken objects are cleaned, bound, studied, and repaired with bone tools, resin, river clay, woven fibre, and quiet magic.

Some relics are restored so their purpose can be known. Others are repaired only enough to prove they should never be used again.

Kelshara is unusually open to human scholars, provided they work through recognised hosts and respect Veylrath ruin law. A small number of human historians, appraisers, translators, and restoration workers live here under clan protection or long-standing scholar agreements.

Seraya Venn is one such human scholar, known locally as a careful relic historian whose work with preserved artifacts has earned more trust than most outsiders receive.

Scholar Law¶

Kelshara does not welcome treasure hunters, but it does welcome serious study.

Visitors may copy inscriptions, examine restored objects, consult oral histories, and work beside local appraisers when permission has been granted. What they may not do is remove relics from protected ruins, conceal dangerous finds, or treat old places as abandoned property.

The settlement’s storytellers teach that memory is not harmless. Remembering the wrong thing too proudly can wake what should have stayed buried.
