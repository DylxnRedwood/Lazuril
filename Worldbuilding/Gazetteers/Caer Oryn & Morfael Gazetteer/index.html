Caer Oryn & Morfael Gazetteer¶

Player-facing gazetteer for the Vermilion Crown's capital and its river-edge satellite town.

Caer Oryn is not a city that lets visitors forget history. Its red stone walls, temple bells, river chains, and candlelit courts all speak of a state built after catastrophe and kept alive by ceremony, suspicion, and trade. It is the capital of the Vermilion Crown, a seat of law and religion where noble houses, temple authorities, merchant interests, and foreign envoys all compete beneath the same weather-dark towers.

Morfael lies beyond the capital's heavier walls, close enough to feel Caer Oryn's pull and far enough to keep its own habits. It is a working town of ferries, river mud, shrine bells, fish smoke, carters, drovers, and gossip. Travellers often pass through Morfael before they ever see the full height of the capital, and many learn quickly that the Crown's laws do not stop at the gates.

This gazetteer is written for players. It contains public knowledge, common assumptions, visible institutions, local atmosphere, and rumours that characters might reasonably hear. It does not include hidden histories, DM-only explanations, encounter tables, or secret campaign information.

At a Glance¶

Caer Oryn¶

Region: The Vermilion Crown
Role: Capital city, crown seat, pilgrimage centre, river fortress, legal and religious hub
People: Mostly human, with aasimar bloodlines, elven envoys, merchants from other states, tabaxi travellers from the eastern forests, dwarven artisans, and a steady flow of pilgrims
Government: Crown authority expressed through magistrates, noble councils, temple courts, chartered guilds, and appointed ward officers
Defence: Crown soldiers, riverwardens, gate officers, temple wardens, and private household retainers
Commerce: River tolls, dyed cloth, court contracts, temple services, parchment, salt, smoked fish, worked stone, glassware, horses, legal scribal work, and pilgrimage trade
Religions: Kelemvor has a strong public presence. Selûne, Chauntea, Corellon, Gond, Tymora, and other recognised gods maintain shrines or chapels. Suspicion falls heavily on secretive cults and shadowed rites.

Morfael¶

Region: Outer reach of Caer Oryn's authority
Role: River crossing, market town, ferry station, drover stop, shrine halt, and first point of contact for many travellers approaching the capital
People: Ferrymen, fishers, carters, charcoal burners, reed-cutters, smallholders, chapel attendants, Crown clerks, retired soldiers, and innkeepers
Government: A town reeve, chapel officers, guild representatives, and Crown-appointed watch officers
Defence: Local watch, river patrols, toll guards, and occasional detachments from Caer Oryn
Commerce: Ferry fees, smoked fish, reeds, charcoal, livestock, market grain, leather, boat repair, candles, road provisions, and gossip bought over ale
Religions: Kelemvor's shrine is respected by travellers, mourners, and soldiers. Chauntea's rites are common among farmers. Selûne's name is spoken by night travellers, riverfolk, and those who dislike moonless roads.

History of Caer Oryn¶

Before the Crown¶

The land around Caer Oryn was inhabited long before the Vermilion Crown gave it walls and law. Older peoples used different names for the river, the hills, and the red stone ridges beneath the city. Some of those names survive in songs, shrine inscriptions, and the speech of families who claim they were here before the Ark made landfall.

To most citizens, the oldest history is not neatly divided into dates. It is remembered as a chain of broken ages: the time before the Ark, the founding settlements, the years of disputed saints, and the long settling of the Crown. Public histories rarely agree on every detail, but they do agree that Caer Oryn became powerful because it stood where roads, river traffic, pilgrimage routes, and political necessity all crossed.

Founding the Capital¶

Caer Oryn began as a fortified height above the river and grew into a capital by absorbing the roads around it. Watch posts became gates. Storehouses became markets. Shrine paths became processional streets. The early rulers of the Vermilion Crown understood that control of a river crossing could become control of food, soldiers, law, and memory.

The city's oldest surviving walls are built from dark red stone quarried from the surrounding ridges. In sunlight, they appear almost warm. After rain, they darken to the colour of old blood. This stone gave Caer Oryn much of its visual identity and helped the city cultivate its reputation as a place where authority is meant to be seen.

The War of the False Saints¶

The War of the False Saints left marks throughout the Crown. In Caer Oryn, its memory survives in closed shrines, defaced reliefs, forbidden feast days, and family names that still make careful people lower their voices. The public lesson is simple: faith and power can destroy a city when neither is held accountable.

In the centuries after the war, Caer Oryn became far more cautious about religious authority. Temples could be honoured, but they were watched. Prophets could be heard, but not blindly obeyed. Miracles could inspire awe, but claims of divine command became a matter for magistrates, scriptors, and temple courts.

The Scriptor Compact¶

The Scriptor Compact reshaped how records, doctrine, temple law, and inter-state religious disputes were handled. Full elven scriptors trained under ancient traditions became a stabilising presence in human religious institutions. To common citizens, a scriptor is not merely a clerk. A scriptor is a witness, a legal memory, and sometimes the only person in a room who can tell a prince, priest, or magistrate that their version of events will not stand.

In Caer Oryn, scriptors are respected, resented, and watched in equal measure. Their seals can settle inheritance, expose forged doctrine, clarify temple obligations, or halt a legal proceeding until older records are consulted.

History of Morfael¶

Morfael was never designed as a grand settlement. It grew where it was useful. Ferries needed landing stages. Drovers needed a place to rest animals before the capital road. Pilgrims needed a shrine before entering Caer Oryn. Fisherfolk needed smoke sheds. Soldiers needed a watch post. Merchants needed a place where goods could be sorted, taxed, hidden, repaired, or argued over before they reached the city.

Older residents insist Morfael has saved Caer Oryn from itself more times than the capital would ever admit. When plague, fire, riots, river floods, or court closures interrupted city life, Morfael kept moving food, candles, carts, fish, bodies, rumours, and letters. The town's pride is practical rather than noble. It does not claim to be beautiful. It claims to endure.

Morfael's name now carries the weight of a threshold. For some travellers, it is the last ordinary place before the politics of Caer Oryn. For others, it is the first sign that the Crown's reach is real.

Caer Oryn Today¶

Caer Oryn today is a city of steep approaches, red walls, crowded courts, temple bells, and heavily watched gates. It is wealthy, but not carefree. Its streets contain courtly splendour and hard poverty within walking distance of each other. Processions pass beggars. Pilgrims kneel beside tax collectors. Magistrates drink in private rooms above markets where forged papers and stolen relics change hands.

The capital's authority rests on a careful balance. The Crown needs the temples, but fears religious excess. The temples need the Crown, but resent political interference. Merchants need stability, but profit from loopholes. Noble houses defend tradition while quietly buying influence wherever tradition fails them. Foreign envoys praise Caer Oryn's order in public and complain about its delays in private.

For adventurers, Caer Oryn is a dangerous kind of opportunity. Work is never difficult to find. Honest work is harder. Legal work is harder still, unless sponsored by a temple, guild, house, magistrate, or military officer.

Morfael Today¶

Morfael is muddy, watchful, and much more important than it first appears. Its people know who has come downriver, who has gone missing, which noble coach arrived without its crest showing, and which priest paid too much for a private ferry after dark. Information moves through Morfael faster than official letters.

The town has a reputation for blunt speech. Caer Oryn citizens often call Morfael rustic. Morfael residents call Caer Oryn expensive, slow, and full of people who need three seals to answer a simple question.

Morfael is not lawless. The Crown has officers there, and the riverwardens take their duties seriously. Even so, the town has more shadows than its size suggests. Smugglers use reed beds. Deserters seek work under false names. Pilgrims disappear into cheap lodging. Travellers who think Morfael is merely a stopover often learn that the town notices everything.

Government and Authority¶

The Crown in Caer Oryn¶

The Vermilion Crown governs from Caer Oryn through layered authority. Few decisions are simple. A matter involving land might involve a noble steward, a guild notary, a temple witness, a magistrate, and a scriptor before anyone with a sword becomes involved.

The Crown's public face is order. Its working reality is negotiation. Noble families control money, marriages, old land rights, and private retainers. Magistrates control warrants, courts, punishments, and public records. Temples control oaths, funerary legitimacy, charitable networks, and moral pressure. Guilds control labour, trade standards, and the quiet ability to make a district stop functioning for a day.

Morfael's Reeve and Watch¶

Morfael is overseen by a town reeve who answers to Crown authority while also surviving local opinion. A reeve who ignores Caer Oryn is removed. A reeve who ignores Morfael is made useless. The post requires patience, memory, and a willingness to be hated by everyone for different reasons.

The local watch handles theft, drunken violence, ferry disputes, missing persons, and toll trouble. Serious crimes, religious accusations, foreign incidents, or matters involving noble retainers are escalated to Caer Oryn.

Temple Courts and Scriptor Witnesses¶

Temple courts are not above Crown law, but they matter. Oaths, funerary rights, inheritance disputes, accusations of false sanctity, and desecration cases can all draw temple attention. When a scriptor is present, their written account may carry more weight than the testimony of a dozen frightened witnesses.

Citizens respect scriptors because their records are difficult to corrupt. Citizens resent scriptors because their records are difficult to escape.

Law, Order, and Protection¶

Caer Oryn's law is formal, written, and unevenly enforced. A poor thief and a noble fraudster may both be guilty, but they will not necessarily experience justice in the same way. Public punishments are still used for visible crimes, especially theft from temples, assault against officers, forged seals, illegal toll evasion, and violence during processions.

Morfael's justice is quicker and more personal. A known troublemaker may be dragged before the reeve before breakfast. A stranger with good papers may still find every door closed if the town believes they are dangerous.

Crown Soldiers¶

Crown soldiers guard gates, walls, courts, storehouses, and official convoys. They are disciplined in public and less predictable in taverns. A soldier in Caer Oryn is expected to know who outranks them. A soldier in Morfael is expected to know when the mud is lying.

Riverwardens¶

The riverwardens inspect boats, ferry ledgers, cargo seals, and suspicious night crossings. They are often more feared than gate guards because they know how people move when they do not want to be seen.

Temple Wardens¶

Temple wardens protect shrines, funerary houses, relic stores, holy records, and pilgrims. Their authority varies by district, but few sensible citizens start a fight beneath temple bells.

Private Retainers¶

Noble houses and wealthy guilds employ private guards. These retainers do not have the same authority as Crown officers, but in practice their uniforms open doors, close mouths, and complicate arrests.

Neighbours, Rivals, and Allies¶

The Court of Thalorien¶

Caer Oryn depends on elven scriptorial expertise while remaining uneasy about elven patience and influence. Thalorien envoys are treated with respect, but the city's human authorities dislike being reminded that some records outlive kingdoms.

The Martarië Crown¶

The Martarië Crown provides cultural prestige, arcane scholarship, courtly exchange, and occasional irritation. Caer Oryn respects Maerithal's refinement but distrusts any power that can make politics sound like art.

The Republic of Havenor¶

Havenor is admired for civic ambition and criticised for republican arrogance. Merchants from Havenor move through Caer Oryn constantly, bringing goods, contracts, lawyers, and arguments about taxation.

The Windmere Assembly¶

Windmere traders are welcome when grain, timber, horses, and practical goods are needed. They are less welcome when their assemblies start asking why Crown permits take so long and cost so much.

The Leridian Concord¶

The Concord's negotiators are patient, polished, and difficult to read. Caer Oryn values them as intermediaries and distrusts them for exactly the same reason.

The Veylrain Expanse¶

People from the Veylrain Expanse are often treated as outsiders by city officials who understand roads better than forests. Tabaxi travellers, clan messengers, herbalists, scouts, and guides sometimes pass through Morfael and Caer Oryn, where they are watched with curiosity, suspicion, or commercial interest depending on the district.

Districts of Caer Oryn¶

Redcourt Heights¶

Redcourt Heights is the political heart of Caer Oryn. Crown offices, noble residences, formal gardens, petition halls, and guarded archives cluster here behind clean stone and controlled access. Nothing in Redcourt Heights is accidental, including who is seen entering which doorway.

The Pilgrim Stair¶

A long rising district of shrines, hostels, candle sellers, oath houses, and mourning chapels. Pilgrims arrive with hopes, grief, bargains, and secrets. The Pilgrim Stair is one of the few places where a noble and a beggar might kneel on the same stone, though they rarely leave by the same door.

Lantern Quays¶

The quays are noisy, wet, crowded, and essential. Barges, ferries, cargo cranes, fish stalls, ropewalks, toll offices, river patrols, and dockside taverns fill the air with shouted prices and gull cries. Anyone looking for passage, cargo work, smuggled goods, or rumours eventually comes here.

Cinderglass Market¶

Cinderglass Market is famous for stained glass, lamp oil, coloured panes, mirrored devotional charms, and expensive window work for temples and noble estates. At night the district glows beautifully. During the day it smells of ash, polish, hot metal, and debt.

The Bonegate Ward¶

The Bonegate Ward stands near funerary roads and older burial grounds. It contains coffin makers, grave clerks, mourning houses, bone carvers, quiet inns, and the practical businesses that gather around death. Outsiders often find it grim. Locals find it honest.

Ashwater¶

Ashwater is a working district of laundries, dye runoff, tanners, cheap food, hard labour, and narrow lanes. It is poor, but not passive. Guild organisers, local toughs, shrine kitchens, and neighbourhood elders all compete to decide who speaks for the district.

Stonehush¶

Stonehush contains older record houses, sealed courtyards, scriptor lodgings, translation rooms, and small libraries attached to temples or noble estates. It is one of the quietest parts of the city. The silence is enforced as much by habit as by law.

The Low Avenues¶

The Low Avenues are the city's crowded spill of workshops, cheap lodging, labour yards, food sellers, and minor shrines. New arrivals often start here. Some rise quickly. Others disappear into the machinery of the city and are remembered only by unpaid room ledgers.

Places of Morfael¶

The Ferry Steps¶

The Ferry Steps are Morfael's busiest place. Boats cross, cargo is argued over, travellers wait in bad weather, and ferrymen pretend not to hear half of what they hear. Anyone asking questions in Morfael usually begins here and is overcharged for the privilege.

Bellwether Green¶

Bellwether Green is the town's open market space, used for livestock inspection, seasonal fairs, public notices, small punishments, and arguments that everyone claims not to enjoy. The green's bell is rung for fire, flood, missing children, and Crown orders.

The Reedcut¶

The Reedcut is a stretch of wet ground, narrow paths, reed stacks, fish traps, and hidden boat-pulls. It provides honest work and dishonest cover. Locals know where the mud holds and where it lies.

Old Kiln Lane¶

Old Kiln Lane is lined with charcoal sheds, cracked brick ovens, potters, lime burners, and families who smell permanently of smoke. It is a good place to find repairs, bad lungs, and people who saw something at dawn but need persuading to say so.

Shrine of the Last Road¶

This small shrine is dedicated to safe passage, final farewells, and the dignity of the dead. Travellers leave candle stubs here before taking the road to Caer Oryn. Soldiers often stop here before deployment. The shrine is plain, but rarely empty.

Redwater Watch¶

Redwater Watch is a Crown post overlooking the river bend. Its officers inspect cargo, question suspicious travellers, and keep lists of boats that travel after dark. The post is too small for the town's problems and too visible for the town's criminals to ignore.

Tallowbridge¶

Tallowbridge is less impressive than its name suggests: a low crossing near candle makers, tallow vats, and cramped workshops. It stinks in warm weather, freezes badly in winter, and is one of the best places in Morfael to hear what working people actually think.

The Mooring Yards¶

The Mooring Yards repair small boats, replace rope, dry nets, and patch cargo skiffs. Strangers looking for quiet passage without official attention often ask too many questions here.

Economy and Trade¶

Caer Oryn's economy is built on authority as much as goods. Courts need scribes. Temples need candles. Pilgrims need beds. Noble houses need servants, guards, architects, tutors, glass, horses, tailors, and lawyers. Guilds turn this demand into money.

The city exports finished goods, official paperwork, legal expertise, dyed cloth, glasswork, carved stone, religious objects, and trained specialists. It imports food, timber, livestock, metals, luxury goods, parchment, rare pigments, and political problems.

Morfael's economy is smaller and more physical. It moves what the capital consumes: fish, reeds, charcoal, grain, livestock, ferry traffic, cart repairs, road provisions, and cheap lodging. It also moves information. The town has no formal guild of rumour brokers, but it does not need one.

Guilds, Crews, and Local Power¶

Caer Oryn has chartered guilds for most respectable trades and informal crews for everything else. A charter gives a guild legal standing, tax obligations, inspection rights, and the ability to ruin independent competitors. An unchartered crew gives people protection, credit, contacts, and a shared willingness to lie for each other.

Important city interests include the glassworkers of Cinderglass Market, the ferrymen of Lantern Quays, the funerary trades of Bonegate, the parchment sellers of Stonehush, the carters who control gate traffic, and the lamplighters who know which streets go dark before trouble begins.

Morfael has fewer charters and more understandings. The ferrymen, reed-cutters, carters, fish smokers, and innkeepers may argue constantly, but they close ranks when Caer Oryn treats the town as a convenience rather than a community.

Religion¶

Religion in Caer Oryn is public, political, and watched. The city remembers what happens when sanctity becomes a weapon. Temples are permitted, honoured, funded, taxed, inspected, and sometimes quietly feared.

Kelemvor¶

Kelemvor's faith has a strong presence because death, oath, burial, judgement, and proper endings matter deeply to the Crown. The Final Journey is one of the best-known Kelemvorite institutions in the city, associated with funerary dignity, lawful passage of the dead, and serious-minded clergy who dislike spectacle.

Selûne¶

Selûne is honoured by travellers, night watchmen, riverfolk, sailors, mothers, dreamers, and those who fear the dark road. Her shrines are often smaller than Kelemvor's institutions, but their lamps are well tended.

Chauntea¶

Chauntea's rites are strongest beyond the walls, especially among farmers, millers, drovers, and food sellers. In Morfael, her blessings are practical: good grain, healthy animals, safe births, and weather that does not ruin everything at once.

Corellon and the Elven Gods¶

Elven visitors and scriptors maintain private rites and small formal spaces rather than grand public temples. Human citizens often respect these traditions without understanding them.

Gond and the Craft Gods¶

Cinderglass Market, the workshops, and the better-funded guildhalls maintain shrines to craft, invention, and precision. A cracked lens, failed hinge, or collapsed scaffold is just as likely to produce a prayer as a curse.

Shadowed Worship¶

Caer Oryn does not ban every unpleasant god by name, but secretive rites, coercive cults, false miracles, and hidden priesthoods draw swift attention. Accusing someone of shadow worship is serious. Proving it is harder. Being wrong can ruin a life.

Citizenry and Culture¶

Caer Oryn citizens are used to ceremony. They know when to stand aside for a procession, when to lower their voice near a shrine, when not to ask about a sealed litter, and when a polite clerk is more dangerous than an angry guard.

Morfael residents value usefulness. A person who can mend a wheel, pole a ferry, read a flood, calm a horse, carry a body, or keep a secret will be accepted faster than someone with impressive manners and no practical skill.

Aasimar lineages exist within the Crown, often treated with fascination, expectation, envy, or resentment depending on family history. Elves are respected for memory and suspected of distance. Tabaxi visitors are often exoticised by city folk and judged more fairly by people who have actually depended on a scout, hunter, or messenger. Dwarven artisans are valued where stone, metal, and discipline matter. Half-elves often move between social spaces more easily than most, though rarely without cost.

Customs Newcomers Notice¶

Visitors to Caer Oryn quickly learn that bells matter. A temple bell, court bell, fire bell, gate bell, and mourning bell all carry different meanings. Locals can often identify the source by tone and timing.

Seals also matter. A paper without a seal may be ignored. A seal without a witness may be challenged. A witness without standing may be dismissed. A scriptor's seal can change the entire mood of a room.

In Morfael, names matter less than introductions. A stranger who arrives vouched for by a ferryman, shrine keeper, or innkeeper can move easily. A stranger with no local connection may be watched until they leave.

Adventurers in Caer Oryn and Morfael¶

Adventurers are useful, distrusted, and rarely unemployed. Caer Oryn offers work through magistrates, temples, guilds, noble houses, foreign envoys, and desperate private citizens. The work may involve escorting pilgrims, recovering stolen records, investigating forged seals, protecting witnesses, finding missing persons, clearing road threats, or carrying messages that official channels cannot safely handle.

Morfael offers rougher work: river searches, missing boats, night noises in the reeds, cargo theft, deserters, dangerous animals, old shrine trouble, and travellers who arrived but never reached the capital road.

The safest adventurers in the Crown have a patron, a written contract, and someone respectable willing to admit they hired them. The most profitable adventurers often lack at least one of those things.

Public Rumours and Common Talk¶

The Crown is said to keep more records than it can read and more secrets than it can bury.

Some residents claim the oldest stones beneath Caer Oryn were already carved before the first Crown banner flew.

Morfael ferrymen insist the river carries voices in flood season. Sensible people blame echo, drink, grief, or all three.

A red-stained dawn is considered unlucky by pilgrims on the Pilgrim Stair, though scholars insist the superstition has several contradictory origins.

Everyone in Caer Oryn complains about taxes. Everyone also wants the gates guarded, the roads patrolled, the bells maintained, the dead buried properly, and the courts open when they need them.

Tone and Use at the Table¶

Caer Oryn should feel formal, layered, beautiful, suspicious, and burdened by history. It is a city where law matters, but power decides how quickly law moves.

Morfael should feel grounded, muddy, intimate, and watchful. It is a place where ordinary people know more than officials expect and where the first sign of a larger story may wash up at the edge of the river.

Together, Caer Oryn and Morfael form a useful campaign gateway: the capital for politics, temples, records, and power; the town for rumours, river trouble, working people, and the first cracks in official certainty.
